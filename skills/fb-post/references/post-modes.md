# Post intentions

One feed-post skill handles these intentions. The structure is a choice,
not a required mold. Ask what the reader should get before choosing an opening.

| Intent | Develop | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conversation/question | Enough context for a considered answer; a question tied to a real decision or experience | Empty 'agree?' prompts, fishing for keyword counts |
| Story | Scene, consequential choice, outcome, honest meaning | Fabricated vulnerability, stolen experience, forced moral |
| Education | Concrete problem, explanation/example, actionable takeaway | Withholding the answer until someone comments |
| Authority/observation | A defensible perspective grounded in experience or cited evidence | Unsubstantiated certainty or borrowed credibility |
| Build-in-public | What changed, what was tried, what is known, what remains | Claiming an unfinished experiment succeeded |
| Case study | Situation, intervention, measured result, scope, permissions | Fake client wins; confusing attribution with causality |
| Experiment | Hypothesis, approach, observation, limitation, next step | Calling one observation universal proof |
| Offer/promotion | Who it helps, specific value, substantiated proof, clear next action | Invented urgency or guaranteed results |
| Video-support | Honest viewing promise, necessary context, optional prompt | Caption disconnected from the video |
| Link | Useful standalone framing plus why the destination is worth visiting | Misleading preview; universal 'link in first comment' rule |
| Image/sequence | Inspectable subject, readable visual hierarchy, useful per-image information and supporting copy | Assuming organic Facebook behaves like LinkedIn PDFs |

For business relevance, connect the lesson to an offer only when the brief calls
for it and the connection is natural. An expertise-demonstrating post can build
trust without a pitch. For growth, recurring subject matter should support a
real editorial promise, not identical openings repeated through a calendar.

## Original example, hypothetical

User material: 'Our test onboarding form had eleven fields. Three testers told
us they couldn't tell which ones were required. We marked the required fields
and are running a second test. No conversion result yet.'

Possible post: 'Three people hit the same problem in our onboarding test:
they couldn't tell which fields they had to fill in. We had eleven fields and
no clear distinction. We've marked the required ones and are testing again.
The lesson so far isn't that shorter forms convert better. It's that our form
asked people to guess. Where does your process make someone guess?'

The question is relevant to the example. It does not manufacture conversion
proof, request a reaction, or require a follow CTA.
